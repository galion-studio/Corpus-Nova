# Why We Run on This Hardware

The evolutionary energy base of *Homo sapiens*: how a big brain, a small fast gut, a cooking fire, a sweating runner's body, and a few gene-dosage tweaks add up to one energy strategy.

## Abstract

> Read the human body as a spreadsheet and it stops looking like a random collection of parts. Compared with the other great apes, humans burn more energy per day (Pontzer et al. 2016a), run a brain of about 86 billion neurons that takes roughly a fifth of resting energy (Herculano-Houzel 2012), and do it on a gut that is smaller and less devoted to fermentation (Milton & Demment 1988; Aiello & Wheeler 1995). The bill is paid by three kinds of upgrade. **Income:** cooking, tools, and dense foods raise the net energy of each bite and shrink feeding time from a predicted ~48% of the day to under 5% (Carmody & Wrangham 2009; Organ et al. 2011). **Efficiency:** walking costs about 75% less than chimpanzee walking (Sockol et al. 2007), and an endurance-running, sweat-cooled body covers ground in heat (Bramble & Lieberman 2004; Lieberman 2015). **Buffering:** humans carry more body fat than other apes (Pontzer et al. 2016a), and the classic "small gut paid for the big brain" trade looks, across mammals, more like a brain-versus-fat trade (Navarrete et al. 2011). The enlarged budget is also **guarded**: size-adjusted daily expenditure rises with activity and then plateaus, so the body reallocates rather than overspends (Pontzer et al. 2016b). On top of that hardware run recent software patches: extra salivary amylase gene copies in high-starch populations (Perry et al. 2007) and lactase persistence alleles that spread within the last ~10,000 years (Bersaglieri et al. 2004; Tishkoff et al. 2007). A co-evolved microbial fermenter still supplies a slice of energy, about 10% by classic estimates (Bergman 1990; Moeller et al. 2016). Pregnancy and lactation are the largest single entries in the female ledger, and they appear to set human gestation length more than the pelvis does (Butte & King 2005; Dunsworth et al. 2012). This paper turns that story into map layers: head (brain cost), abdomen (gut shift), skin and thorax (heat dump), lower limb (endurance), pelvis (reproductive budget).

## 1. The hook: you are an energy strategy

**Answer first:** the human body is best read as the physical record of an energy strategy. Earn more energy per hour, spend less on moving and digesting, store a buffer, and put the surplus into brains and babies.

Most origin stories are skeleton stories. A skull gets rounder, a pelvis gets bowl-shaped, a foot gets an arch. Those changes are real, but they skip the question that limits every animal: **who pays?** Neurons, fetuses, milk, muscles, and gut walls all run on the same currency, ATP (adenosine triphosphate, the cell's rechargeable energy coin), and ATP comes from food that has to be found, chewed, digested, and absorbed.

So let's run the books. A "great ape" baseline looks like this:

- **Slow and frugal.** Primates as a group burn only about half the daily energy expected for placental mammals of the same body mass (Pontzer et al. 2014). Slow living, slow growth, long lives.
- **Bulky, fibrous diet.** Plant foods make up roughly 87% to more than 99% of the annual diet of wild great apes (Milton 2003).
- **A big fermentation gut.** Food passes slowly, and a large hindgut (colon) lets microbes ferment fiber the ape cannot digest itself (Milton & Demment 1988).
- **Lots of chewing time.** Large-bodied apes spend a large share of their waking day feeding.

Humans broke almost every line of that baseline.

- We burn **more** per day than any other ape, after adjusting for size and activity: roughly 400 kcal/day more than chimpanzees and bonobos, about 635 more than gorillas, and about 820 more than orangutans (Pontzer et al. 2016a). Most of that extra comes from basal metabolic rate (BMR: the energy you burn lying still, awake, after fasting), which means our organs themselves run hotter.
- We carry **more body fat** than other apes (Pontzer et al. 2016a).
- We spend **far less time eating**: about 4.7% of daily activity, versus about 48% predicted for a primate of our body mass and family tree (Organ et al. 2011).
- We spend a remarkable share of our resting energy on **one organ**: the brain.

Four sentences of accounting, and the anatomy starts to make sense. The rest of this paper walks through each line of the ledger and pins it to a region of the body map.

Five ideas carry most of the weight, and each one overturns a simpler story:

1. **The brain was not paid for by the gut alone.** Across mammals, the measurable trade is brain against fat stores (Navarrete et al. 2011), revising the classic expensive-tissue swap (Aiello & Wheeler 1995). → Section 2.
2. **Part of the human gut is outside the body.** Cooking raises the net energy of food before the intestine starts work (Carmody & Wrangham 2009). → Section 3.
3. **The chassis is a run-and-cool machine.** Early *Homo* carries a derived endurance-running body plan from about 2 million years ago (Bramble & Lieberman 2004). → Section 4.
4. **The budget is big but guarded.** Daily burn rises with activity, then plateaus (Pontzer et al. 2016b). → Section 5.
5. **Digestive anatomy includes gene count.** Extra *AMY1* copies track high-starch diets (Perry et al. 2007). → Section 6.

The eureka shelf [`library/evolution/`](../library/evolution/README.md) holds one short card per idea. This paper places them in sequence.

> **Fun fact.** On an energy-per-day basis, the most "unusual" thing about humans may not be any single organ. It is that we turned up the whole furnace while living in a lineage (primates) famous for running cold (Pontzer et al. 2014, 2016a).

## 2. The expensive brain and the trade it forced

**Answer first:** the human brain is costly, about a fifth of resting energy for about 2% of body mass, and something had to give. The original "expensive-tissue" idea said the gut gave. Later comparative work says the bigger, cross-mammal trade is brain versus fat, plus more income and less spending on locomotion. Diet quality is still the hinge.

### What a neuron costs

Brains are expensive because neurons keep their membranes charged. Every signal lets ions rush across the membrane; pumps then burn ATP to push them back. Multiply by tens of billions of cells running all day, including during sleep, and the bill is large.

Suzana Herculano-Houzel's lab counted neurons by dissolving brains into a uniform "soup" of nuclei (a method called the isotropic fractionator). The human brain has about **86 billion neurons** and roughly as many non-neuronal cells (Herculano-Houzel 2012). Her key point: the human brain is not a metabolic freak per neuron. It is a scaled-up primate brain whose energy cost tracks its huge **number** of neurons. The widely quoted "about 20% of the body's energy for about 2% of its mass" holds, but it holds because we have so many neurons, not because each one is unusually greedy (Herculano-Houzel 2012).

Leonard, Snodgrass & Robertson (2007) put the comparative frame on it: humans spend a larger fraction of their energy budget on brain metabolism than other primates. Across primates, relative brain size rises with diet quality (energy and nutrient density per bite), and humans sit at the top end of that line.

The cost is steepest in childhood. Combining PET (positron emission tomography, imaging glucose uptake) and MRI data, Kuzawa et al. (2014) estimated that the brain's glucose use equals about 52.5% (boys) and 59.8% (girls) of resting metabolic rate at birth, then **peaks in childhood** at about 66% of resting metabolic rate. Body growth slows at exactly the ages when brain demand peaks, and picks up again as brain demand falls. Children grow slowly partly because their brains are eating the growth budget.

> **Fun fact.** At the peak, a child's brain is using glucose equal to about two-thirds of the whole body's resting energy (Kuzawa et al. 2014). Children are brains with a body attached, metabolically speaking.

### The expensive-tissue hypothesis (1995)

Leslie Aiello and Peter Wheeler asked the obvious question: if the brain got so big, how did we afford it without a matching rise in basal metabolic rate? Their answer, the **expensive-tissue hypothesis**, focused on the other metabolically "expensive" organs: heart, liver, kidneys, and gut. Their comparison found the human brain much larger than expected for a primate of our size, and the gastrointestinal tract much smaller than expected. Total "expensive organ" mass came out about where a primate of our size would predict: brain up, gut down (Aiello & Wheeler 1995).

The logic chain was clean:

1. A smaller gut is only possible on **higher-quality food**: food that is easy to digest and dense in energy.
2. A smaller gut frees energy that can run a bigger brain.
3. A bigger brain helps find, process, and share higher-quality food.

It's a loop. Better food allowed a cheaper gut, and the energy saved went to a better brain, which got better food. The hypothesis became a textbook staple.

### The 2011 update: the trade is brain versus fat

Ana Navarrete, Carel van Schaik, and Karin Isler tested the idea in 100 mammal species, 23 of them primates, with real organ-mass data. After controlling for fat-free body mass, **brain size was not negatively correlated with digestive-tract mass, or with any other expensive organ** (Navarrete et al. 2011). The specific gut-for-brain swap did not hold up as a general mammalian rule.

What did show up was a negative correlation between brain size and **adipose depots** (fat stores). Large brains and large fat stores look like alternative ways to survive lean times: be smart enough to find food, or carry enough fuel to wait. Humans break the pattern by doing both. Navarrete et al. (2011) suggest that works for us because walking upright is cheap enough that carrying fat does not ruin our locomotor efficiency. They proposed that human brain expansion was paid for by **stabilizing energy income** (cooking, sharing, tools) and **redirecting energy** from locomotion, growth, and reproduction.

Read as engineering, the revision changes which part counts as the spare. Aiello & Wheeler treated the gut as the component you could shrink to power a bigger processor. Navarrete et al. (2011) found that across 100 species the component that trades against the processor is the **energy store**. A lineage can survive lean periods with a large fuel tank or with a large brain that finds food, and most mammals cannot carry both. Humans carry both, so the human design needs a reason the fat tank stays affordable. Two reasons appear later in this paper: cheap bipedal transport (Section 4) and a larger, guarded total budget (Section 5).

So which version should the map show? Both, with honest labels:

- **Robust:** the brain is expensive; diet quality tracks brain size; the human gut is relatively small and built for dense food (Leonard et al. 2007; Milton & Demment 1988).
- **Revised:** the exact organ-for-organ bookkeeping. The gut-for-brain swap is not a general mammal law (Navarrete et al. 2011).
- **Added:** metabolic acceleration. Humans also simply **burn more** (Pontzer et al. 2016a). The budget grew, not just the allocations.

Confidence, as the eureka shelf grades it: **high** that the brain is costly and that diet quality tracks brain size; **medium** on the exact organ-for-organ bookkeeping. The human gut is still small and absorption-first (Section 3). What changed is the claim that its shrinkage alone balanced the books.

### The raw-food ceiling

One more piece makes the brain story tighter. Fonseca-Azevedo & Herculano-Houzel (2012) modeled how many neurons a primate could afford given the calories in **raw** foods and the number of hours in a day available for feeding. Great apes hit a ceiling: a big body *and* a big brain would need more feeding hours than exist. They argued that this limit was probably broken in *Homo erectus* with the shift to a cooked diet. That's the bridge to Section 3: the brain's budget problem is really a **digestion** problem.

## 3. A small, fast gut and the stomach outside the body

**Answer first:** the human gut is a smaller, faster, absorption-first version of the ape gut. The small intestine dominates; the fermentation chamber (colon) is reduced. That design only works because humans moved much of digestion **outside** the body: cutting, pounding, and above all cooking.

![Schematic comparison: in great apes the colon is the largest gut compartment; in humans the small intestine is the largest. Widths show rank order only.](../figures/homo-origins/homo-origins-gut-shift.png)
*Figure 2. Same parts, different budget. Schematic rank order, not measured volumes (Milton & Demment 1988; Milton 1999; Aiello & Wheeler 1995).*

### Same parts list, different floor plan

Human and ape guts share a parts list: stomach, small intestine, cecum, colon. The difference is how volume is divided, and how fast food moves through.

- In great apes, the **hindgut** (cecum + colon) is large. Food lingers. Bacteria ferment fiber into **short-chain fatty acids**, also called volatile fatty acids (VFA: small fats like acetate, propionate, and butyrate that the gut wall absorbs as fuel).
- In humans, total gut volume and hindgut volume are **smaller** than in other hominoids (Milton & Demment 1988), and the small intestine, where enzymes break food down and absorb it directly, takes the largest share (Milton 1999).

Katharine Milton and Montague Demment ran digestion trials on chimpanzees fed high-fiber (34% neutral-detergent fiber) and low-fiber (14%) diets. Chimps responded to fiber much like humans do (more fiber, faster transit, less fiber digested), but their mean transit time was **longer** than humans'. The likely reason is their larger hindgut (Milton & Demment 1988). The physiology is shared; the plumbing is scaled differently.

Milton's synthesis: in plant-eating mammals, bigger bodies usually mean lower-quality diets (think gorilla, leaves and pith). The human lineage broke that rule. It got bigger *and* raised diet quality, by routinely adding **animal-source foods** and using technology and social cooperation (Milton & Demment 1988; Milton 1999, 2003).

*Segment-by-segment anatomy (stomach acid, sphincters, villi, colon segments) is owned by **Gut Energy**. This paper only explains why the overall proportions look the way they do.*

### Cooking as external digestion

Richard Wrangham's argument, made in peer-reviewed form with Nancy Lou Conklin-Brittain, starts with an observation: **no human foraging society has been recorded living without cooking**, and modern people who choose strict raw-food diets report low energy and impaired reproductive function (Wrangham & Conklin-Brittain 2003). That suggests cooking is not a cultural garnish. It may be **obligatory** for our biology. Wrangham's book *Catching Fire* (2009) gives the long-form version; this paper uses the journal papers.

What does heat actually do? Carmody & Wrangham (2009) laid out the mechanisms:

- **Starch gelatinizes.** Heat plus water unpacks tight starch granules so enzymes can reach them.
- **Proteins denature.** Unfolded proteins are easier for digestive enzymes (proteases) to cut.
- **Digestion gets cheaper.** Cooked meat costs less energy to digest than raw.
- **Defense gets cheaper.** Cooking kills food-borne pathogens and breaks down some toxins, cutting the body's spending on detoxification and immune defense.

Pounding and slicing help too, but heat does things pounding cannot: near-complete starch gelatinization, efficient protein denaturing, pathogen killing. So cooking added energy even on top of sophisticated non-thermal processing (Carmody & Wrangham 2009).

How large is the gain? The eureka card [`cooking-energy.md`](../library/evolution/cooking-energy.md) gives cooked-starch net energy gains of roughly **12–35%, depending on the food**, from Carmody & Wrangham (2009). Treat that range as provisional. It has not yet been matched to a specific table in the full text, and the verified source list does not repeat it (`sources/homo-origins-01-sources.md`, "Not verified"). The direction is not provisional. Heat raises net energy, and the mouse feeding trials below measure that directly.

This is the core of the **external gut** idea. A fire is a pre-digestion chamber that sits outside the body and runs on wood instead of on the animal's own ATP. Each step done at the hearth (unpacking starch, unfolding protein, killing microbes) is a step the intestine no longer has to pay for. That is how a lineage could keep a small, fast, absorption-first gut and still feed a large brain. Confidence follows the card: **high** that cooking raises net energy, **medium** on when habitual cooking became fixed in *Homo* (see the timeline below).

The experiment that tested it: Carmody, Weintraub & Wrangham (2011) fed mice meat and sweet potato that was raw or cooked, whole or pounded. **Cooking increased the energy gained from meat**, raising body mass without any change in food intake or activity, and cooking beat pounding for both meat and tubers. Fasted mice preferred cooked food. One sharp side note from the paper: food labels, which assign calories by chemical content, systematically **overestimate** the energy you actually get from poorly processed foods.

> **Fun fact.** Two plates with identical "calories" on the label can deliver different energy to your body, depending on whether the food was cooked, ground, or eaten raw and whole (Carmody et al. 2011). The label measures the food; your body measures what it can extract.

### The stomach outside the body: tools first, fire later

The timeline matters, and it is debated.

- **Feeding time collapsed in the Homo line.** Using phylogenetic comparison across primates, Organ et al. (2011) found modern humans spend about **4.7%** of daily activity feeding, against about **48%** predicted. Molar teeth shrink sharply with *Homo erectus*, beyond what body size predicts, placing the food-processing shift after *Homo* appeared but **before or alongside the origin of *H. erectus*, about 1.9 million years ago**.
- **Tools and meat came first.** Zink & Lieberman (2016) measured chewing in people eating meat and starchy underground storage organs (USOs: tubers, roots, corms). If meat made up one-third of the diet, chewing cycles per year would drop by nearly **2 million (13%)**, and chewing force by 15%. Just slicing meat and pounding tubers cut chews by another 5% and force by another 12%. Their reading: smaller teeth and jaws in early *Homo* were first made possible by **stone tools plus meat**, and cooking became common later (they put that at roughly the last 500,000 years).
- **Fire is old, but how old as a habit is unclear.** The earliest secure in-place evidence of burning at an archaeological site is burned bone and ashed plant remains in Wonderwerk Cave, South Africa, from about **1.0 million years ago** (Berna et al. 2012). That shows fire use; it does not prove daily cooking.

So the honest version is a relay: **tools and meat** (earliest *Homo*) → **reduced chewing and feeding time** (by *H. erectus*) → **fire** (by ~1 Ma) → **habitual cooking** (date debated, likely later). Each step moved more digestion out of the body.

### Starch: the forgotten fuel

Meat gets the headlines, but glucose-hungry tissues (brain, red blood cells, the developing fetus) want glucose, and **cooked starch** is a dense source of ready glucose. Hardy et al. (2015) argued that digestible carbohydrate was essential to the human energy story, that salivary amylase is largely ineffective on raw crystalline starch, and that cooking greatly increases both its energy yield and its effect on blood sugar. They predicted that the spread of cooking and the rise in salivary amylase gene copies (Section 6) should be linked. That is still a testable hypothesis, not a settled timeline.

## 4. Locomotion: cheap walking, endurance running, heat dump

**Answer first:** humans are built to cover long distances cheaply and to shed heat while doing it. Walking costs about a quarter of what a chimpanzee pays, and a suite of springs, stabilizers, and sweat glands makes us poor sprinters but excellent endurance runners in heat.

### Walking: the 75% discount

Michael Sockol, David Raichlen, and Herman Pontzer measured oxygen use in adult chimpanzees walking on two legs and on four, and compared them with humans. **Human walking was about 75% less costly** than chimpanzee walking, whether the chimp walked bipedally or quadrupedally (Sockol et al. 2007). The savings came from anatomy: a more extended hip and a longer hindlimb. Even ape-like early hominins standing a bit straighter could have walked more cheaply than a knuckle-walker.

Why does that matter for energy? Foragers walk a lot. A cheaper kilometer means more ground covered per calorie, a wider foraging range, and, as Navarrete et al. (2011) pointed out, the ability to carry a fat buffer without paying a heavy transport tax.

Pontzer (2017) traced it through the fossils. The earliest hominins were bipedal but kept ape-like hindlimb features that limited walking economy. *Australopithecus* had longer legs, more extended posture, and a stiffer midfoot: improved, more human-like economy, but still limited endurance. The full **endurance** suite (larger limb joints, a spring-like arch) appears in *Homo* in mosaic fashion and is complete only by about **1 million years ago**. Endurance capability rose in parallel with brain size and metabolic rate.

### Running: the endurance specialist

Dennis Bramble and Daniel Lieberman made the case that running, not just walking, shaped the genus *Homo* (Bramble & Lieberman 2004). Humans are poor sprinters compared with most four-legged mammals, but they perform remarkably well at sustained long-distance running. Many of the relevant features leave traces in the skeleton, and the fossil record suggests endurance running is a derived capability of *Homo*, emerging about **2 million years ago**.

![Schematic runner with callouts: nuchal ligament, high sweat-gland density with reduced fur, large gluteus maximus, long legs and springy tendons, short toes and stiff arch, shoulders decoupled from head.](../figures/homo-origins/homo-origins-run-and-cool.png)
*Figure 3. The run-and-cool chassis. Schematic; trait list from Bramble & Lieberman (2004), Lieberman (2015), Kamberov et al. (2018), and Pontzer (2017).*

The traits, grouped by job (Bramble & Lieberman 2004; Pontzer 2017):

- **Springs:** long legs, a long Achilles tendon, and a springy foot arch store and return elastic energy with every stride.
- **Stabilizers:** a **nuchal ligament** (an elastic band at the back of the neck) helps keep the head steady; an enlarged **gluteus maximus** (the big buttock muscle) stabilizes the trunk during running more than walking.
- **Counter-rotation:** a head that is mechanically decoupled from the shoulders lets the arms and torso counter-rotate against the legs.
- **Cheap push-off:** short toes and a stiffer foot.
- **Cooling:** lots of sweat and little fur (next subsection).

In energy terms, the chassis lowers the cost of each kilometre and keeps the engine from overheating in daytime heat. Both matter for the ledger. A forager who can cover long distances in midday heat reaches food that others cannot use, and the spring-loaded legs that make this possible also make carrying a fat buffer affordable (Section 2). The endurance chassis sits on the same timeline as the feeding-time collapse and molar reduction around early *H. erectus* (Section 3; Organ et al. 2011), so the income side and the spending side change together.

How was running used? Lieberman (2015) sketches a sequence. First, bipedal walking for efficient foraging. Then, in hot open habitats, an advantage in dumping heat to forage at midday when predators rest. Later, endurance running for **scavenging and then hunting**, including **persistence hunting** (chasing prey in heat until it overheats). The sequence is a hypothesis with good anatomical support; which scenario dominated is still open. Confidence follows the eureka card: **high** for a derived endurance capability in *Homo*, **medium** for which scenario (scavenging, hunting, or both) did the selecting.

### Heat dump: sweat glands, not missing hair follicles

Running in heat generates a lot of heat. Humans dump it mainly by **sweating**: evaporation from bare skin.

Here's the surprise. Yana Kamberov and colleagues counted hair follicles and eccrine sweat glands (the watery, cooling type) in five skin regions of macaques, chimpanzees, and humans. Humans have **chimpanzee-like hair follicle density**. We did not lose follicles; our hairs got tiny and fine, so fur cover dropped. What jumped is sweat: **eccrine gland density averages about 10 times higher in humans** than in chimpanzees and macaques (Kamberov et al. 2018).

> **Fun fact.** You have about as many hair follicles per patch of skin as a chimpanzee. You are not hairless; you are "fine-haired and sweaty," a cooling upgrade rather than a deletion (Kamberov et al. 2018).

Lieberman (2015) summarizes the end state: all living humans remain adapted for long-distance locomotion rather than speed, and to **dump** heat rather than retain it. Local populations later fine-tuned cold and heat tolerance, but the core chassis is a sweat-cooled distance machine.

## 5. A bigger, guarded budget: metabolic acceleration and constrained spending

**Answer first:** humans did not just reshuffle a fixed ape budget. They **raised** it, burning several hundred kcal/day more than other apes. Then they **guard** it. Across lifestyles, adult daily energy expenditure stays within a fairly narrow band, because the body compensates when activity rises.

### How energy is measured in the wild

Most of the numbers here come from **doubly labeled water** (DLW). A subject drinks water tagged with harmless heavy isotopes of hydrogen and oxygen. Hydrogen leaves the body only as water; oxygen leaves as both water and CO₂. The gap between how fast the two isotopes wash out reveals CO₂ production, and so energy burned, over one to two weeks of normal life. It is the gold standard for **total energy expenditure** (TEE: everything you burn in a day, including resting metabolism, digestion, and movement).

### Primates run cold; humans turned up the heat

Pontzer et al. (2014) measured TEE in 17 primate species, captive and wild. The result: primates expend only about **50%** of the calories expected for placental mammals of their mass. That lines up with primate slow growth and long lives.

Then Pontzer et al. (2016a) compared humans with chimpanzees, bonobos, gorillas, and orangutans using DLW. Humans burned more: roughly **400 kcal/day more than chimpanzees and bonobos, 635 more than gorillas, and 820 more than orangutans**, after accounting for body size and physical activity. The difference was driven mainly by higher **basal metabolic rate**, so it is built into the organs, not just extra activity. Humans also had the **highest body-fat percentage** of the group. The authors' reading: a faster metabolism let humans afford bigger brains and greater reproductive output, and extra fat helped buffer that more expensive engine against shortfalls.

> **Fun fact.** If an orangutan lived inside a human-sized body and ran on orangutan settings, it would burn about the energy of a large meal less than you every single day (Pontzer et al. 2016a).

### The guarded budget: constrained total energy expenditure

If humans evolved to be active, you'd expect very active people to burn far more energy. The data say: more, but less than you'd think.

- **Hadza foragers.** Pontzer et al. (2012) measured TEE in Hadza hunter-gatherers in Tanzania, who walk long distances every day. After controlling for body size, Hadza TEE was **no different** from that of Westerners, despite much higher activity.
- **Five populations, 332 adults.** Pontzer et al. (2016b) measured TEE and activity (with accelerometers) in adults across five populations in Africa and North America. TEE rose with activity at low levels, then **plateaued** at higher activity. The body appears to compensate: more energy for movement means less for other tasks such as immune activity, reproductive hormones, or stress responses. The authors argue this fits better with a **constrained** model of TEE than the simple additive model ("add exercise, add calories").

Read the plateau as a control system rather than a meter. In the **additive** model, daily burn equals a fixed base plus whatever you spend on movement. In the **constrained** model, the total is held within a band, and extra movement is paid for partly by cutting other line items. That changes how the earlier sections read. The extra kcal/day humans burn compared with other apes (Pontzer et al. 2016a) is a larger budget, not a larger allowance for any single item. The brain, the fat buffer, reproduction, and an endurance-running body (Section 4) all draw on that one guarded total. Confidence: **high** for the plateau pattern in this study design; the mechanisms and what it means for public health are still debated.

Constrained TEE is a central idea for this paper because it says the human budget is a **managed ledger**: the body reallocates among line items rather than letting the total run away. It also gives one evolutionary reason why exercise alone tends to be a weak tool for weight loss, while exercise remains strongly tied to health. (Clinical and weight-management detail belongs to sibling themes, not here.)

### Across the life course

Pontzer et al. (2021) analyzed a large, diverse DLW database covering males and females aged 8 days to 95 years. After adjusting for fat-free mass (FFM: everything except fat), TEE rises fast in infancy to about **50% above adult levels** around age one, then declines slowly to adult levels by about age 20. It stays **stable from about 20 to 60**, including during pregnancy, and declines after about 60. Those changes track tissue-level metabolism, not just body size.

That curve lines up with the brain story: the high-cost early years overlap the brain's peak glucose demand (Kuzawa et al. 2014).

## 6. Software patch 1: *AMY1* and starch

**Answer first:** humans carry more copies of the salivary amylase gene, *AMY1*, than chimpanzees. Copy number varies widely between people and tracks salivary amylase levels, and populations with traditionally high-starch diets carry more copies on average. It is a clear example of gene **dosage** responding to diet. Claims linking *AMY1* copy number to obesity are contested.

### Copy number as a dial

Most genes come in two copies, one from each parent. Some regions of the genome come in variable numbers of copies: **copy number variation** (CNV). The *AMY1* gene, which makes salivary amylase (the enzyme in spit that starts breaking starch into sugars), is one of the most famous.

George Perry and colleagues measured *AMY1* copy number and salivary amylase across people and populations (Perry et al. 2007):

- **More copies, more enzyme.** Copy number correlated positively with the amount of amylase protein in saliva.
- **Diet tracks copies.** Individuals from populations with high-starch diets had, on average, more copies than those from low-starch populations. In their samples, about **70%** of people from high-starch populations carried at least six diploid copies, against **37%** of people from low-starch populations.
- **Human lineage gain.** Chimpanzees have **two** diploid copies, with no variation detected. Humans average about **three times** as many copies. Humans also have about **6–8 times** more salivary amylase protein than chimpanzees.

The diet–copy link held across geographically close populations with different diets, which points to diet and selection rather than simple ancestry. This is the main point of the section: **digestive anatomy is not only organs**. In *Homo sapiens* it also includes the number of copies of an enzyme gene, adjusted to local food within a single species. This is one of the clearest cases of selection acting on copy-number variation. Confidence is **high** for the copy-to-protein correlation and the population differences. The selection reading is strong, but it may not be the only explanation for every population. Because higher copy number means more enzyme, the variation likely matters for how starch is handled, but a direct effect of copy number on energy gain was not measured in that study.

How this connects to cooking: raw crystalline starch resists amylase; cooked, gelatinized starch is open to it (Hardy et al. 2015). Extra amylase is most useful when starch is cooked. Hardy et al. (2015) proposed that cooking and *AMY1* expansion co-evolved. That is a testable hypothesis, and the precise date of the expansion remains an open question for this shelf.

> **Fun fact.** Hold an unsalted cracker in your mouth for a minute and it starts to taste sweet. That is salivary amylase cutting starch into sugars, and the dose you carry depends partly on how many *AMY1* copies you inherited (mechanism: Perry et al. 2007).

### The obesity question: a cautionary tale

In 2014, Falchi et al. reported that low *AMY1* copy number was associated with higher BMI (body mass index) and obesity. A year later, Usher et al. (2015) measured copy number precisely, in 1,000 obese or lean Estonians and two further cohorts totaling about 3,500 people, and found **no association** with BMI or obesity despite having about **99% power** to detect the earlier-reported effect size. They also found copy number is usually **even**, reflecting the structure of the gene region.

The lesson for the library: *AMY1* is a clean story about **evolution and diet**, and a messy, contested story about **modern disease**. Cards should say so.

## 7. Software patch 2: lactase persistence

**Answer first:** most mammals, including most humans, lose the ability to digest milk sugar after weaning. In some populations, single-letter DNA changes in a switch near the lactase gene keep it on into adulthood. Those variants spread fast, within the last ~10,000 years, and arose independently in Europe, Africa, and elsewhere. Why they spread is being re-argued.

![Timeline from ~2 million years ago to today: endurance traits and molar reduction around H. erectus, burning at Wonderwerk ~1.0 Ma, habitual cooking later (debated), and lactase persistence alleles sweeping within the last ~10,000 years; AMY1 copy number tracks starch diets in living populations.](../figures/homo-origins/homo-origins-timeline.png)
*Figure 4. Deep time to recent patches. Dates are estimates from the cited sources; "cooking becomes common" is shown as debated.*

### The default: switch off after weaning

Lactase-phlorizin hydrolase (LPH, usually just "lactase") sits on the brush border of the small intestine and splits lactose into glucose and galactose, which can then be absorbed (Enattah et al. 2002). In most mammals, and in most humans, lactase activity declines after weaning (Gerbault et al. 2011). Losing lactase is the ancestral default. **Lactase persistence** (LP) is the derived, newer trait.

### The switch: an enhancer upstream of *LCT*

Enattah et al. (2002) found a variant, C/T-13910, about **14 kb upstream** of the *LCT* gene. The "T" version is strongly associated with lactase persistence in Finnish families and beyond. It does not change the enzyme; it changes the **switch** that controls how much enzyme gets made. That's why LP is a regulatory story.

Bersaglieri et al. (2004) showed the haplotype carrying the T allele is long and common (about 77% in their European-derived samples), a sign of a recent **selective sweep**. They estimated strong selection within the last **5,000–10,000 years**, placing it among the strongest selection signals in the genome.

### Convergent evolution in Africa

Tishkoff et al. (2007) genotyped 470 Tanzanians, Kenyans, and Sudanese and found the European variant rare there. Instead they found **three different variants** (G/C-14010, T/G-13915, C/G-13907) associated with lactase persistence, in the same enhancer region. The G/C-14010 haplotype showed a sweep beginning about **7,000 years ago**. Different human groups, different mutations, same outcome: **convergent evolution** driven by a shared culture (herding and milk drinking).

Ségurel & Bon (2017) summarize: **five** variants are known to be associated with LP; persistence is largely **monogenic** (one main variant) in Eurasia but mostly **polygenic** in Africa. Several puzzles remain, including how widely the trait spread and why.

### Where and why

Itan et al. (2009) used simulation to estimate that the European −13910*T allele began undergoing selection about **7,500 years ago**, in a region between the central Balkans and central Europe, probably among early farming cultures. Their simulations did not support the idea that the main driver was extra vitamin D/calcium at high latitudes.

Gerbault et al. (2011) frame LP as a **gene–culture niche construction** case: dairying created the environment; the environment selected the gene; the gene reinforced dairying.

The most recent big update, from Evershed et al. (2022), cuts against the simplest version. Analyzing milk-fat residues in nearly **7,000** pottery fragments from over 550 archaeological sites, they found milk use was **widespread in Europe from the Neolithic**, long before LP was common. In UK Biobank data, LP genotype had only a **weak** link to milk consumption today. Their models suggest LP selection is better explained by **famine and pathogen exposure**: in hard times or epidemics, milk drinkers without lactase suffer more from diarrhea and dehydration, so the allele mattered most when life was on the edge.

The energy framing for this paper: LP turns milk into a reliable **adult** energy and fluid source, and the selection was likely sharpest when other food failed.

*Clinical lactose intolerance, testing, and the fermented-dairy reintroduction how-to belong to **Gut Energy**. Fermented products (yogurt, cheese) lower lactose; that's why prehistoric non-persistent people could still use milk.*

## 8. The co-evolved fermenter: microbes as an energy organ

**Answer first:** human gut microbes are partly inherited lineages that have traveled with hominids for millions of years. They still convert fiber we cannot digest into fuel, but in humans that fermentation supplies a modest share of energy, about 10% in classic estimates, compared with most of the energy in cows. Modern diets may be shrinking the fermenter.

### How much energy comes from fermentation?

Edward Bergman's review of volatile fatty acid (VFA) production across species put the numbers side by side: VFA supply roughly **70%** of the energy of ruminants (cows, sheep), about **20–30%** for many other herbivores and omnivores, and about **10%** for humans (Bergman 1990). That fits the anatomy in Section 3: a smaller hindgut means a smaller fermentation vat. The microbiome is still a real organ in the energy budget, just a smaller one in us. It also produces signals and vitamins that matter for physiology (detail owned by Gut Energy and BioChem).

### Microbes that came along for the ride

Andrew Moeller and colleagues compared gut bacteria across humans, chimpanzees, bonobos, and gorillas. Some bacterial families, including **Bacteroidaceae** and **Bifidobacteriaceae**, show family trees that mirror the host family trees. In other words, they **cospeciated** with hominids over roughly the last **15 million years** (Moeller et al. 2016). Parts of your microbiome are lineages your ancestors carried before humans and chimps split.

Earlier work by Moeller et al. (2014) found the human gut microbiome has changed **faster** than those of other apes since the lineages split, has **lost** some ancestral diversity, and has become relatively specialized toward diets richer in animal foods.

### Foragers: a reference, not a museum

Living foragers are not "frozen ancestors," but they show what microbiomes look like outside industrial diets.

- **Hadza (Tanzania).** Schnorr et al. (2014) found Hadza gut microbiomes had **higher taxonomic richness and diversity** than urban Italians, no detectable *Bifidobacterium*, and enrichment for groups like *Prevotella* and *Treponema*. They also found differences between Hadza men and women that track their different foods (sexual division of labor in foraging).
- **Seasons.** Smits et al. (2017) analyzed 350 Hadza stool samples across a year and found microbiome composition **cycles with the seasons**, with certain "volatile" taxa disappearing and reappearing. Those same taxa are rare or absent in industrialized populations.

### The extinction ratchet

Erica Sonnenburg and colleagues fed mice carrying a human-derived microbiome a diet low in **MACs** (microbiota-accessible carbohydrates, mainly plant fibers that microbes can eat). Diversity dropped. Restoring fiber within a generation partly rescued it, but over **several generations** the losses compounded and became harder to reverse without reintroducing the missing taxa (Sonnenburg et al. 2016). This is evolution in miniature: a co-evolved partner can be lost faster than it was gained.

*How to rebuild a microbiome (diet, fermented foods, clinical evidence) is owned by **Gut Energy**. This paper only gives the deep-time frame.*

## 9. Reproduction and sex layers: the largest line items

**Answer first:** pregnancy and lactation are among the largest energy costs in the human life cycle. Human babies are born with big brains and a lot of body fat, and gestation length seems to be set more by the mother's metabolic ceiling than by the width of the birth canal. Male and female bodies differ on average in lean and fat tissue, and those differences shift with puberty, pregnancy, and menopause. Average is not destiny: the variation within each sex is large.

### What a pregnancy costs

Butte & King (2005) modeled the energy costs from measured data. For a healthy pregnancy with about 12 kg gestational weight gain:

- **Total cost:** about **321–325 MJ** (roughly 77,000 kcal) over the whole pregnancy.
- **By trimester:** extra intake of about **375, 1,200, and 1,950 kJ/day** (about 90, 290, and 470 kcal/day) in the first, second, and third trimesters.
- **Lactation:** average milk output about **749 g/day** during exclusive breastfeeding to six months, at about 2.8 kJ/g. Total cost about **2.62 MJ/day** (about 625 kcal/day). After subtracting about 0.72 MJ/day mobilized from stored tissue (mostly fat laid down in pregnancy), the net extra need is about **1.9 MJ/day** (about 450 kcal/day).

Note how those numbers interact with Section 5: when Pontzer et al. (2021) adjusted TEE for fat-free mass, adult expenditure stayed stable even in pregnancy. The extra energy of pregnancy is largely the cost of **more tissue** (mother and baby), not a faster engine per kilo.

### Why the baby is born when it is: energetics versus the pelvis

The classic "obstetric dilemma" says human gestation is cut short because a wider pelvis would make walking inefficient, so babies are born early to fit through the birth canal. Dunsworth et al. (2012) tested its assumptions. They found no good evidence that a wider pelvis would compromise locomotion, and showed that human gestation is actually **longer** relative to maternal size than in other apes. They proposed the **energetics of gestation and growth** (EGG) hypothesis: birth happens when the fetus's energy demand approaches the mother's **metabolic ceiling** (the maximum she can sustain).

Warrener et al. (2015) added a direct measurement: in men and women walking and running, **pelvic width did not predict** locomotor cost. Men and women were equally economical. The pelvis is not the hard limit the dilemma required.

(Childbirth mechanics and pelvic shape are not dismissed; they are owned by sibling themes. This paper only weighs the energy side.)

### Big brains, fat babies

Human newborns are unusually fat for mammals. Cunnane & Crawford (2003) noted that human babies have brain and body fat **each** around **11–14%** of body weight, and argued that fat stores act as a fuel and building-material reserve for the fast-growing brain. Their wider argument, that shoreline foods rich in specific fatty acids drove human brain evolution, is a **hypothesis** and remains debated; the paper uses only the infant composition point and labels the rest as hypothesis.

Dufour & Sauther (2002) reviewed primate and human reproductive energetics: long gestation and long lactation spread costs out, lowering daily demand, and **human females carry larger fat stores** than females of other primates. Strategies to cover the costs (eating more, storing more, moving less, lowering metabolic rate) vary between populations and seasons.

### Sex layers: shared plan, different averages

The body map has three layers: **shared**, **male**, and **female**. Each has equal depth.

- **Shared:** everything in Sections 1–8 applies to all humans. The brain cost, small gut, cooking, endurance chassis, *AMY1*, LP, and microbiome have no sex-specific rule.
- **Puberty is the main switch.** Wells (2007) reviewed body composition: sex differences are modest in early childhood and emerge mainly at **puberty**, driven by sex steroid hormones. On average, males gain more lean mass and bone; females gain more fat, especially in **peripheral** (hips, thighs, breast) depots.
- **Life stages shift it again.** Parity (having children) and menopause move female fat distribution toward a more central (**android**, around the abdomen) pattern (Wells 2007).
- **Populations vary.** Wells (2012) analyzed 96 non-industrialized populations: sexual dimorphism in lean mass and in fat showed a trade-off and **varied with environment**. It was greater in colder and Arctic settings and lower in many African and Asian populations. Body composition is plastic, not fixed.
- **Locomotion:** equally economical by sex (Warrener et al. 2015).

> **Not destiny.** Every sex difference here is an **average** with wide overlap. The map shows the averages as layers and the overlap as a statement on every card.

*How fat is built, stored, and signals (adipose biology) is owned by **Adipose**. This paper only places the reproductive budget in evolutionary context.*

## 10. Synthesis: one strategy, five map layers

**Answer first:** the human body is the result of one energy strategy with three levers: **more income**, **cheaper spending**, and **a bigger buffer**. The surplus went into neurons and offspring. Each lever has an anatomical address, and those addresses become the five Homo Origins map layers.

| Lever | What changed | Main anatomy | Key sources | Map region |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Income | Tools, meat, cooking, starch; feeding time ~4.7% of day | Small teeth and jaw; small, fast, small-intestine-first gut | Carmody & Wrangham 2009; Organ et al. 2011; Zink & Lieberman 2016; Milton & Demment 1988 | head (mouth), abdomen |
| Income patch | *AMY1* dosage; lactase persistence | Salivary glands; small intestine brush border | Perry et al. 2007; Bersaglieri et al. 2004; Tishkoff et al. 2007; Evershed et al. 2022 | head, abdomen |
| Efficiency | Walking ~75% cheaper; endurance springs; sweat cooling | Hip, legs, tendons, arch, gluteus, nuchal ligament, eccrine glands | Sockol et al. 2007; Bramble & Lieberman 2004; Kamberov et al. 2018 | lower limb, thorax/skin |
| Budget size | TEE above other apes; constrained total | Whole-body BMR; organs | Pontzer et al. 2016a, 2016b, 2021 | whole body |
| Buffer | Highest ape body fat; brain-vs-fat trade | Adipose depots; infant fat | Navarrete et al. 2011; Pontzer et al. 2016a; Cunnane & Crawford 2003 | abdomen, pelvis (→ Adipose) |
| Spending: brain | ~86 billion neurons, ~20% of resting energy; child peak ~66% | Brain | Herculano-Houzel 2012; Kuzawa et al. 2014 | head |
| Spending: offspring | Pregnancy ~321–325 MJ; lactation net ~1.9 MJ/day | Uterus, placenta, breast | Butte & King 2005; Dunsworth et al. 2012 | pelvis, thorax (female layer) |
| Partner | Co-evolved microbiome, ~10% of energy | Colon | Bergman 1990; Moeller et al. 2016 | abdomen |

### Five things that are robust

1. **Humans run a bigger budget than other apes**, measured directly with DLW (Pontzer et al. 2016a).
2. **The human gut is reduced in fermentation capacity** and depends on high-quality, processed food (Milton & Demment 1988; Wrangham & Conklin-Brittain 2003; Carmody et al. 2011).
3. **Humans are built for economical distance locomotion and heat dumping** (Sockol et al. 2007; Bramble & Lieberman 2004; Kamberov et al. 2018).
4. **Daily expenditure is constrained, not additive**, in the measured populations (Pontzer et al. 2012, 2016b).
5. **Salivary amylase dosage tracks starch diets** (Perry et al. 2007).

### Four things that are still argued

1. **When cooking became habitual.** Fire by ~1.0 Ma (Berna et al. 2012); feeding-time shift by ~1.9 Ma (Organ et al. 2011); routine cooking possibly much later (Zink & Lieberman 2016). Cards label this as debated.
2. **The exact trade that paid for the brain.** Gut-for-brain (Aiello & Wheeler 1995) versus fat, locomotion, and income (Navarrete et al. 2011; Pontzer et al. 2016a). Most likely several at once.
3. **Why lactase persistence spread so fast**: milk as everyday food, or milk as a famine/pathogen survival advantage (Itan et al. 2009; Evershed et al. 2022; Ségurel & Bon 2017).
4. **What the endurance chassis was for.** The capability is solid; whether scavenging, persistence hunting, or both drove it is not settled (Bramble & Lieberman 2004; Lieberman 2015).

### What "evolutionary base" does and doesn't license

This paper explains **why** the hardware looks the way it does. It does **not** say ancestral diets are automatically healthy, that any single forager group is a template, or that averages describe individuals. Modern health, clinical gut questions, and diet advice belong to the sibling themes, which can use this as their deep-time frame.
