# Blood buffers (bicarbonate)

**One-liner:** Your blood holds its pH almost still by trading CO₂ and bicarbonate. Think of it as a chemical shock absorber with two hands on the controls.

**In plain words**
- Metabolism makes acid all day.
- **Bicarbonate** (HCO₃⁻) soaks up that acid and turns into CO₂ + water, using the enzyme carbonic anhydrase.
- **Lungs** breathe the CO₂ away in minutes.
- **Kidneys** rebuild bicarbonate and dump acid over hours to days.

**Key numbers**
- Arterial pH **7.35–7.45**. The whole band is only about a 25% swing in H⁺.
- HCO₃⁻ ≈ **24 mM**; CO₂ pressure ≈ **40 mmHg** (≈ 1.2 mM dissolved). Their ratio is **20:1**, and pH = 6.1 + log(20) ≈ **7.4**.
- Inside cells, pH is ~7.2. Lysosomes run at ~4.5–5, and the mitochondrial matrix at ~7.8–8 (Casey et al. 2010).

**Why a "bad" buffer works:** a pKa of 6.1 should be a poor match for pH 7.4. The trick is that the system is **open**: lungs and kidneys keep resetting both ends.

**Why it matters:** small pH drifts change protein charges and hemoglobin's grip on O₂.
